    Ethics & boundaries quick reference

    The six principles, the "whose benefit" test, and the most common gray areas — one page, meant to sit open next to a real decision, not read start to finish.

    The core test: if this choice benefits me — my comfort, my ego, my loneliness, my finances, my need to be liked or needed — more than it benefits the client's treatment, it doesn't belong in this relationship, however good it feels in the moment.

    The six principles (ACA Code of Ethics)

    Autonomy: respect the client's right to self-direction, including choices you disagree with.
    Nonmaleficence: above all, do no harm — including harm that doesn't look like harm at first.
    Beneficence: actively promote the client's wellbeing, not just avoid hurting them.
    Justice: treat clients fairly and equally — no favorites, no exceptions based on who you like.
    Fidelity: honor your word, your role, and the trust the client placed in you.
    Veracity: be truthful with clients, including about the limits of your role.

    Dual relationships

    • Romantic or sexual contact with a current client: absolute bar, no exceptions, ever.
    • Friendships, business dealings, social media connections with current clients: avoid — the power differential doesn't disappear because it's friendly.
    • Small-town/rural overlap is not a special exception — it raises the bar on transparency and supervision, not a reason to relax the rule.

    Self-disclosure test

    • Ask first: whose need does this serve — the client's treatment, or my own?
    • Keep it brief, purposeful, and immediately redirected back to the client.
    • If you feel a pull to disclose because you want to be understood, liked, or seen as relatable — that's the signal to hold back, not share.

    Gifts & favoritism

    • Follow your program's gift policy exactly — when in doubt, decline warmly and explain why.
    • Notice favoritism in small things: extra time, softer consequences, inside jokes — justice means every client gets the same standard.

    Crossing vs. violation

    • Boundary crossing: a deviation that's still defensible, transparent, and documented — not automatically harmful.
    • Boundary violation: exploitative, harmful, and serves the clinician over the client.
    • The slope runs one direction: undisclosed crossings normalize the next, larger one. Bring crossings to supervision before they compound.
